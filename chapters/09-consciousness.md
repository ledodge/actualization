# Chapter 9: Actualization vs. Experience

### The consciousness question

---

## The intuition

This is the claim the whole book started with. **Consciousness is actualization.** It isn't thought or awareness. It's the process of possibilities becoming definite, happening at every scale.

I made it a matter of degree using what I called the **radiation model**: the more often a system actualizes, the more it "radiates" consciousness.

- **Human brains** are dense and fast-changing, so they actualize at a very high rate. That's why thought feels like something.
- **Rocks** actualize slowly and simply. They're conscious, but only faintly.
- **AI systems**, which I thought worked in superposition, wouldn't radiate at all.

Chapter 6 already removed the AI claim. This chapter checks the rest.

---

## The check

### The rock problem

Chapter 1 previewed this, and it's the most important single test the framework failed.

If consciousness is the *rate* of actualization, I need a physical quantity that measures that rate. The closest real one is the **decoherence rate** from Chapter 6: how quickly a system's quantum possibilities get recorded in its environment.

Decoherence rates grow with a system's **size** and with how strongly it **interacts with its surroundings**. A large rock sitting in air, bathed in light and constantly hit by gas molecules, decoheres at a staggering rate, at least as fast as a brain of similar mass. **[E]**

So, by my own definition, a big rock is at least as conscious as you are. That's not a small adjustment. It means **actualization rate can't be what makes brains special.**

### What does make brains special?

Brains aren't distinctive because things happen in them often. They're distinctive in how they **process information**: they integrate signals from many sources, build models of the world and of themselves, and feed outputs back in as inputs. A rock does none of that.

That points to existing work.

**Integrated Information Theory (IIT)**, developed by Giulio Tononi from 2004 onward, proposes that consciousness corresponds to how much a system's information is **integrated**: how much the whole carries beyond what its parts carry separately. It uses a quantity called Φ (phi). **[F/P]** IIT is controversial, and many researchers doubt it, partly because it implies some simple but highly integrated systems would be conscious. But it's a serious, mathematical theory, and it's in the same family as where my framework now points.

**Global Workspace Theory** (Bernard Baars, Stanislas Dehaene) proposes that consciousness arises when information is broadcast widely across the brain, making it available to many processes at once. **[E/F]**

### A test that separates the two ideas

Here's where this gets testable. **Anesthesia** makes people unconscious, but it barely changes how fast their brains decohere: the brain stays warm, wet, and full of activity at the molecular level.

- If consciousness tracked **actualization rate**, anesthesia shouldn't affect it much.
- If consciousness tracks **integration**, anesthesia should reduce it sharply.

Researchers have measured something close to the second. The **perturbational complexity index (PCI)**, developed by Marcello Massimini, Adenauer Casali and colleagues (2013), sends a magnetic pulse into the brain and measures how complex and widespread the echo is. PCI is high in waking people and in dreaming sleep, and it drops sharply under anesthesia and in dreamless sleep. **[E]** It's even used to assess patients who can't communicate.

The evidence favors integration over actualization rate.

### The redefinition problem

There's a deeper problem with "consciousness is actualization," and it isn't about any experiment.

When people say "consciousness," they usually mean **experience**: the fact that there's *something it is like* to be you, to see red or feel pain. The philosopher David Chalmers called explaining this the **hard problem**: even a complete physical account of the brain seems to leave open why any of it is accompanied by experience. **[P]**

If I define consciousness as "anything becoming definite," I haven't explained experience. I've changed the subject and kept the word. That can be legitimate, but I have to say it openly, and I have to stop implying that I've solved something I haven't.

### Panpsychism: the honest version of my claim

Underneath the redefinition there's a real philosophical position: **panpsychism**, the view that some form of experience is a basic feature of matter, not something that appears only in brains. Philosophers including Philip Goff and Galen Strawson defend it seriously. **[P]**

Panpsychism faces a known challenge called the **combination problem**: if electrons have tiny experiences, how do they combine into one unified experience like yours, and why don't rocks have one? An integration-based approach suggests an answer: experience combines only in systems that integrate information as a whole. Brains do; rocks don't. That's the version of my idea I can defend.

### What about Penrose and Hameroff?

The closest existing theory to my original claim is **Orchestrated Objective Reduction (Orch OR)**, from Roger Penrose and Stuart Hameroff. It proposes that consciousness arises from gravity-induced quantum collapse inside tiny structures in neurons called microtubules. **[E/F]**

The main objection came from Max Tegmark (2000). He estimated that quantum states in a warm, wet brain would decohere in around 10⁻¹³ seconds or faster, far too quickly to play the role Orch OR needs. Supporters have disputed parts of his estimate, and research on quantum effects in biology continues. But most neuroscientists remain unconvinced, and anyone pursuing my original framework needs to engage with Tegmark's argument first.

---

## What survives

My original claim splits into two, and they need to be kept apart.

1. **Actualization** (physics): possibilities become recorded facts through decoherence and redundancy. This happens everywhere, in rocks as much as in brains. **[E]**
2. **Experience** (philosophy, with some testable parts): subjective experience may correspond to how a system **integrates** information, not to how often it actualizes. This puts me close to IIT and panpsychism. **[P/F]**

- **The radiation model is withdrawn.** Actualization rate doesn't track consciousness, and the rock problem shows it. **[E]**
- **Anesthesia and PCI favor integration over actualization rate.** **[E]**
- **Calling actualization "consciousness" renames the hard problem without solving it.** **[P]**

The rest of the book uses the two words separately: **actualization** for the physics, **experience** for the philosophy.

---

**What would change my mind about this chapter:** evidence that consciousness does track some *rate* of physical events, in a way integration measures can't explain, or a version of the rock problem that actualization rate can survive.
