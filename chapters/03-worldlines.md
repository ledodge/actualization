# Chapter 3: Worldlines and the Space of Possibilities

### Paths, fields, and what a "curve" really is

---

## The intuition

The last chapter ended with a correction: the "curve or string" I imagined each of us traveling on is a worldline in spacetime, and "all the paths we might take" belongs to a different space, the space of possibilities. This chapter is about what happens to the rest of my original idea once those two are separated.

Here's the idea in its original form. I pictured each person's life as a string stretching through time. A higher observer could see the string's current direction and all the ways it might continue. Strings that pass close to each other influence each other. I compared this to **electrostatics**: put several sheets of charge near each other and the potential around them takes on a complicated shape, with each sheet bending the field felt by the others. My guess was that our lives work like that in 4D. The effects are *calculable* in principle, but in a complicated enough situation the *outcome* isn't predictable.

I also had a sense that a person could "move their string," that at any moment we might change direction in a way the higher observer couldn't foresee.

There are three separate claims tangled together here:

1. Our paths are curves that can be described and calculated.
2. Paths that come close influence each other, the way charges do.
3. Even so, outcomes can't be predicted, because we can change direction.

The first two hold up well, and physics has precise language for them. The third splits into a physics question (this chapter and the next) and a philosophical one (flagged at the end).

---

## The check

### Worldlines have structure: light cones

A worldline isn't free to go anywhere. In relativity, nothing travels faster than light, and that one rule gives every point in spacetime a shape around it called a **light cone**. **[E]**

Picture a spacetime diagram with time running up the page. From any event, light spreads outward in every direction, tracing a cone that opens upward into the future. A matching cone opens downward into the past.

- Everything **inside your past light cone** could have influenced you.
- Everything **inside your future light cone** is something you could influence.
- Everything **outside both cones** is causally disconnected from you, at least for now. Nothing there can affect you, and nothing you do can affect it, until enough time has passed for light to cross the gap.

So the "strings interact when they're close" intuition needs a sharper statement. Two worldlines can influence each other only through what's inside their light cones, and influence always travels at or below light speed. The relevant kind of closeness is in *spacetime*, not just in space.

### Choice and the block universe

Here's where my "the string can change direction" idea hits a real problem.

Relativity has an awkward feature: observers moving at different speeds disagree about which distant events are happening "now." That's called the **relativity of simultaneity**, and it's been confirmed thoroughly. **[E]** Some philosophers of physics have argued that it implies the **block universe**: past, present, and future all exist equally, and every worldline is already drawn from end to end. On that view, no string ever "changes direction." It just has the shape it has.

Others disagree. Some argue for a **growing block**, in which the past and present are real and the future gets added as time goes on. Others argue that relativity only describes what's measurable, and leaves open what time is fundamentally like. **[P]**

My original intuition quietly assumed both pictures at once. A higher observer can *see* our whole curve, which is the block view. Yet we can still *change* it, which requires an open future. I have to pick one, or at least admit the question is unsettled. For the rest of the book, I'll assume that **the future isn't fixed for any observer who exists inside the universe**, and I'll mark wherever that assumption matters. Whether it's ultimately true is a philosophical question physics hasn't decided.

### The space of possibilities is a real tool

Leave spacetime for a moment and go back to the third kind of space from Chapter 2: the space of possibilities.

In classical mechanics, the full state of a system at one instant is a single point in **phase space**. Phase space has one axis for every position and one for every momentum. For a single ball rolling on a table, that's four numbers: two positions and two momenta. For a gas of a trillion molecules, it's six trillion numbers. As time passes, the point moves, and its path through phase space is the system's whole history. **[E]**

Two facts about these paths matter for this book:

1. **For deterministic laws, paths in phase space never cross.** If two paths met at the same point, they'd have identical states and would have to continue identically. So each state has exactly one future. **[E]** That's the precise version of "a higher observer who knows the current state can see where you're headed."
2. **The space is enormous.** A human body has something like 10²⁸ atoms. The phase space of a person has more dimensions than there are grains of sand on Earth, by a vast margin. **[E]** When I imagined a higher observer seeing "every path we might take," this is the space they'd have to see.

### Quantum mechanics: all paths really do count

There's one place where "seeing every possible path" turns out to be literally part of established physics, though not in the way I first imagined.

In quantum mechanics, Richard Feynman showed that the probability of a particle getting from A to B can be calculated by adding up a contribution from **every possible path** between them, not just the straight one. Wild, zigzagging paths are included. Most of them cancel each other out, and what's left is concentrated around the path classical physics predicts. This is the **path integral**, and it's one of the standard ways of doing quantum physics. **[E]**

So the universe, in a sense, *does* "consider" every path. The path we observe is the one that survives the cancellation. I want to be careful here: the path integral is a calculation method, and physicists disagree about whether those other paths are "real" in any deeper sense. **[P]** But it's a striking match for the intuition, and it's where Chapter 6 picks up.

### Interaction: the electrostatics picture, corrected

Now the charges.

My original analogy used sheets of charge, and that needs fixing right away. The electric field from an infinite flat sheet of charge is **uniform**: it's the same strength no matter how far away you are. **[E]** Moving two infinite sheets closer together doesn't make them interact more strongly. If I want "closer means more influence," I need point charges or small objects, where the force falls off with distance (as 1/r² for point charges).

The deeper correction is about what "interacting strings" means. When two systems interact, the useful picture isn't two separate curves bending each other. It's **one curve in a bigger space**. Combine your phase space and mine into a single larger space, and the two of us together are one point moving through it. The interaction shows up as a **potential energy landscape** in that combined space: hills and valleys that steer the point. **[E]** Two charges near each other make a deep valley or a steep hill in the combined landscape, depending on their signs. Far apart, the landscape flattens.

That gives a precise version of my intuition:

- "Our strings influence each other more when they're close" becomes: **the coupling term in the combined potential is stronger when the systems are close.**
- "The shape of each string depends on everyone else's" becomes: **separate descriptions stop being exact once systems interact.** The honest description is the combined one.

This last point turns out to matter a lot. In Chapter 5 I'll show how information theory measures how much two interacting systems have become *correlated*. In quantum mechanics this goes further, into **entanglement**, where the combined state can't be split into two separate states at all.

### "Calculable but not predictable" is real, but electrostatics isn't the cause

The core of my original idea was that effects can be calculated, but outcomes in complicated situations can't be predicted. That's true, and it's one of the most important results in modern physics. But electrostatics isn't where it comes from.

A static arrangement of charges is completely predictable: given the charges, the field is fixed, and you can calculate it to any precision. Unpredictability appears when things **move and interact at the same time**. The classic example is three bodies orbiting each other under gravity. The laws are simple and fully deterministic, and there's still no general formula for where the bodies will be, because tiny differences in starting conditions grow until they dominate. This is **chaos**, and it's the subject of the next chapter.

## What survives

- **Each of us really does trace a curve: a worldline in spacetime.** Its possible influences are limited by light cones. **[E]**
- **"Seeing someone's whole curve" assumes a block universe, and "changing direction" assumes an open future.** These conflict. Physics hasn't settled which is right; I assume an open future and say so. **[P]**
- **The space of possibilities is phase space.** For deterministic laws, each state has exactly one future, so knowing the state means knowing the path. **[E]**
- **"All paths contribute" is literally true in quantum mechanics** through Feynman's path integral, though whether those paths are "real" is debated. **[E/P]**
- **Interacting systems are best described as one point in a combined space**, steered by a potential landscape whose coupling grows with closeness. This replaces my sheets-of-charge picture. **[E]**
- **Calculable-but-unpredictable is real, and it comes from chaos, not electrostatics.** That's Chapter 4.

What about the higher beings who might nudge our strings, like someone guiding a 2D creature with a pencil? I haven't forgotten them. That idea is philosophical rather than physical, so it has its own clearly labeled chapter: Chapter 12.
