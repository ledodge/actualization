# Chapter 7: Why Facts Stick

### Irreversibility, redundancy, and "collapse resistance"

---

## The intuition

Once something has actualized, why doesn't it just flip back into superposition, or into some other outcome, a moment later?

My answer was **collapse resistance**. Some collapses go "deeper" than others and resist being undone. I compared this to a ball in a potential well: the deeper the well, the harder it is to climb back out. I applied it at every scale:

- **Indecision** would be a shallow collapse, easily reversed.
- **Conviction, commitment, willpower** would be deep collapses, resisting reversal.
- **Mass and gravity** might be how we *perceive* this resistance, the way magnetism is how we perceive the magnetic field.

The gravity idea was the boldest part of the original framework. This chapter checks all three.

---

## The check

### Physics already answers the question

"Why doesn't a collapse reverse?" has a good answer in established physics, and it builds directly on Chapter 6.

When a quantum system decoheres, information about its state doesn't go to one place. It gets copied into enormous numbers of photons, air molecules, and other bits of environment, and those copies spread outward. To *undo* the outcome, you'd have to track down every one of those copies and reverse every interaction exactly. **[E]**

That's not forbidden by the laws of motion, which are reversible. It's forbidden by **statistics**. The number of ways the copies could be scattered is so large that reassembling them is unimaginably unlikely. It's the same reason a broken egg doesn't reassemble or spilled cream doesn't separate back out of coffee: the second law of thermodynamics. **[E]**

### Quantum Darwinism: depth as redundancy

Wojciech Zurek developed this idea into a framework he calls **quantum Darwinism** (summarized in *Nature Physics*, 2009). **[E/F]**

The core insight: a fact becomes "objective," meaning many independent observers can agree on it, when the environment holds **many redundant copies** of it. You learn the position of your coffee cup by intercepting a tiny fraction of the photons bouncing off it. Someone else intercepts a different fraction. Neither of you disturbs the cup, and you agree because you're reading different copies of the same record.

Zurek gives a precise measure, **redundancy**: how many independent fragments of the environment each carry full information about the fact. Experiments with photons and quantum dots have started measuring it directly.

That is the precise version of my "collapse depth":

- **A shallow fact** has few copies in the world. It's fragile and easily disturbed.
- **A deep fact** is copied redundantly everywhere. Reversing it would take an impossible amount of coordination.

So "collapse resistance" doesn't need a new field or force. **Resistance = redundancy of records + the statistics of irreversibility.** **[E/F]**

### Gravity isn't collapse resistance (as stated)

Now the boldest claim: that mass and gravity are how we perceive collapse resistance.

In Einstein's general relativity, gravity is the curvature of spacetime produced by energy and momentum. It's one of the most precisely tested theories in physics, confirmed by everything from GPS satellites to gravitational waves. **[E]** Mass, meanwhile, already has a meaning as resistance: **inertia**, resistance to changes in motion. The equivalence principle says inertial mass and gravitational mass are the same, tested to better than one part in 10¹⁴.

For "gravity = collapse resistance" to count as physics, it would need to reproduce all of that. As stated, it doesn't make any prediction general relativity doesn't already make, and it isn't connected to redundancy, which is what resistance turned out to be.

There *is* serious work linking gravity and collapse, though, and I should point to it rather than reinvent it. **Roger Penrose** and **Lajos Diósi** proposed that gravity *causes* collapse: a superposition of a mass in two places creates two incompatible spacetime geometries, and nature resolves the conflict by collapsing, faster for bigger masses. **[E/F]** As Chapter 6 described, the simplest version has been ruled out, and refined versions are being tested now. That's the closest real-world relative of my intuition, and it runs in the opposite direction: gravity triggering collapse, not collapse appearing as gravity.

### Willpower and attractors

What about conviction and indecision as deep and shallow wells?

There's a legitimate scientific model that looks a lot like this, but it comes from neuroscience, not quantum mechanics. **Attractor networks**, introduced by John Hopfield in 1982, model memory and decision-making as a state rolling downhill into one of several valleys in a landscape. **[E/F]**

- A **shallow valley** is an unstable state. A small nudge pushes the network into a different one. That looks like indecision.
- A **deep valley** is a stable state. It takes a big push to dislodge. That looks like a firm memory or a committed decision.

These models are used to study real neural data, including how the brain holds a decision in working memory. So "willpower as well depth" survives, but as a model of neural dynamics, not as a property of wavefunction collapse.

---

## What survives

- **Facts stick because their records are copied redundantly**, and undoing them would require reversing an astronomically large number of interactions. **[E]**
- **"Collapse depth" has a precise, measurable version: redundancy**, from Zurek's quantum Darwinism. **[E/F]**
- **"Mass and gravity are perceived collapse resistance" doesn't survive as stated.** General relativity explains gravity, and mass already means inertia. **[E]**
- **The real link between gravity and collapse is Penrose and Diósi's work**, which proposes the reverse relationship and is being tested experimentally. **[E/F]**
- **Willpower as a deep well survives as neuroscience**, through attractor networks. **[E/F]**

Irreversibility doesn't only make facts stick. It also gives time a direction. That's Chapter 8.

---

**What would change my mind about this chapter:** a phenomenon where facts stay fixed even though their records are *not* redundant, which would mean something besides redundancy provides resistance.
