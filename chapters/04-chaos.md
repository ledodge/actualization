# Chapter 4: Calculable but Unpredictable

### Chaos, and the limits of seeing ahead

---

## The intuition

A higher observer watching our lives, I thought, could see the curve each of us is on and every direction it might go, but couldn't know *when* we'd change course. That seemed to explain something about prediction in general. People who seem able to "read" where someone's life is headed are often right and sometimes badly wrong. Good guesses beat bad guesses, but nobody guesses perfectly.

My explanation was that our curves interact in ways that can be *calculated* in principle but can't be *predicted* in practice once enough of them are involved.

Chapter 3 showed that electrostatics, my original analogy, doesn't produce this kind of unpredictability. This chapter is about what does.

---

## The check

### Determinism isn't predictability

In 1814 Pierre-Simon Laplace imagined an intellect that knew the position and velocity of every particle in the universe. With Newton's laws, he argued, it could calculate the entire future. Nothing would be uncertain.

For deterministic laws, Laplace is right *in principle*. Chapter 3 noted that paths in phase space never cross, so each exact state has exactly one future. **[E]**

The catch is the word *exact*.

### The butterfly effect

In 1961 the meteorologist Edward Lorenz was rerunning a simple weather simulation. To save time, he typed in numbers from a printout that were rounded to three decimal places instead of six. The new run tracked the old one for a while, then drifted until the two forecasts had nothing to do with each other. A difference in the fourth decimal place had taken over completely. He published the analysis in 1963, and it became the founding example of **chaos theory**. **[E]**

A chaotic system is one where small differences in starting conditions grow *exponentially*. The growth rate is measured by the **Lyapunov exponent**. Roughly, if two nearly identical starting states differ by some tiny amount, that difference doubles every fixed interval of time. After enough doublings, it's as large as the whole system. **[E]**

That sets a **predictability horizon**: the time after which even a nearly perfect measurement of the present is useless for forecasting. **[E/F]**

- For weather, the horizon is about two weeks. Better computers don't push it out much, because each improvement in measurement precision buys only a little extra time.
- For the planets of the solar system, it's millions of years. Over billions of years, even planetary orbits become unpredictable.
- For three stars orbiting each other, it can be very short.

Because the horizon grows only with the *logarithm* of measurement precision, making a measurement a thousand times more precise adds only a fixed, modest amount of forecast time.

### Chaos is everywhere complex things interact

Chaos needs surprisingly little: a few interacting parts and some nonlinearity, meaning effects that aren't simply proportional to their causes. A double pendulum, two pendulums hinged together, is chaotic. So is a dripping faucet. Brains, ecosystems, economies, and social groups all have far more interacting parts than that. **[E]**

So my intuition was right that complicated interacting systems resist prediction, and it was right that this happens even when every individual interaction is simple and calculable. The mechanism is exponential sensitivity, not anything special about higher dimensions.

### Quantum randomness: unpredictable even in principle

Chaos is unpredictability in *practice*. Quantum mechanics adds unpredictability in *principle*.

When a radioactive atom decays, or a photon hits a half-silvered mirror, standard quantum mechanics says there's no hidden fact that decides the outcome in advance: only probabilities. Experiments testing **Bell's theorem**, recognized with the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics, rule out a large class of hidden-variable explanations. **[E]**

Combine the two and you get something important. Chaotic systems amplify tiny differences. Quantum events supply tiny differences that nothing could have predicted. Given enough time, a chaotic system can magnify a single quantum event into a macroscopic difference. **[E/F]** Whether brains actually do this in any way that matters is an open research question.

### What about "changing direction"?

My intuition said the higher observer can't predict *when we change direction*. Physics offers two precise versions of that:

1. **Chaotic version:** our direction was determined, but by details too fine for any observer to measure. **[E]**
2. **Quantum version:** some of our "turns" trace back to events that weren't determined by anything. **[E/F]**

Neither is the same as **free will**, the idea that *we* choose the turn. If the turn is determined by microscopic details, it isn't up to us in the usual sense. If it's random, it isn't up to us either. Philosophers have argued about how free will fits with either picture for centuries. **Compatibilists** argue that choice means acting on your own reasons and values, which is consistent with determinism; others disagree. **[P]** I'm not going to settle that here, and I'll be honest that physics doesn't settle it either.

---

## What survives

- **Calculable but unpredictable is real.** It's called chaos, and it comes from exponential sensitivity to starting conditions. **[E]**
- **Every chaotic system has a predictability horizon** set by its Lyapunov exponent. Beyond it, even a near-perfect observer can only give probabilities. **[E]**
- **This applies to any observer, including a hypothetical higher one**, unless it can measure the present with literally infinite precision. **[E/F]**
- **Quantum randomness adds unpredictability in principle**, and chaos can amplify it. **[E]**
- **Whether "changing direction" is genuine choice** is the free will question, and it's philosophy. **[P]**

The predictability horizon is the idea I'll return to most. It's the precise version of "good guesses beat bad guesses, but no guess is perfect," and Chapter 10 uses it to look at clairvoyance and forecasting.

---

**What would change my mind about this chapter:** evidence that biological systems systematically *suppress* chaos so well that human behavior is predictable far beyond the horizons I've described.
