# Chapter 11: Morality as an Emergent Group Property

### Where my framework met social science

---

## The intuition

I proposed that morality is neither purely **subjective** (just opinion) nor **universal** (absolute rules written into the cosmos). Instead it's a **structural property** that emerges from collective "resistance patterns" at the scale of groups.

That seemed to explain several things:

- Moral frameworks differ across scales: what's right for an individual, a family, or a society isn't always the same.
- People act differently in groups, as if a group-level field pulls on each person's choices.
- Violence isn't *caused* by high-resistance ideas. Some systems are simply drawn toward violent outcomes, because for them it's the path of least resistance.

Alongside this, I thought **language already encodes the physics**. Words for "attractive" states, like love, calm, intuition, and inspiration, describe low-resistance states. Words for "repulsive" states, like anger, distrust, conflict, and confusion, describe high-resistance states. I suggested this wasn't a metaphor: the physics of collapse produces the experiences those words describe.

---

## The check

### The core claim is defensible, and already studied

"Morality emerges from group-level patterns rather than being purely subjective or absolute" is a serious position, and there's a large body of research supporting versions of it. **[E/P]**

**Game theory.** In 1980, the political scientist Robert Axelrod ran computer tournaments of the **iterated prisoner's dilemma**, a game where two players repeatedly choose to cooperate or betray. The winning strategy, **tit-for-tat**, was simple: cooperate first, then copy whatever the other player did last. Across many rounds, cooperation emerged and stabilized without any central authority imposing it (*The Evolution of Cooperation*, 1984). **[E]**

**Cultural evolution.** Robert Boyd and Peter Richerson have shown how norms (shared rules about fairness, punishment, and cooperation) spread and stabilize within groups and compete between them. Different groups settle on different norms, which fits my observation that moral frameworks vary by scale and by group. **[E]**

**Group behavior.** That people act differently in groups is well documented. Solomon Asch's conformity experiments in the 1950s showed people agreeing with an obviously wrong group answer. Research on **deindividuation** shows how anonymity in crowds can change behavior. **[E]**

So my intuition lands in established territory, and that's good. It means the claim is testable and has been tested.

### The physics layer adds nothing

The problem is the "resistance" framing. Once the claim is stated in terms of game theory and cultural evolution, the collapse-resistance language doesn't add any prediction or explanation. **[P]** I can't name an observation that the "group resistance field" version predicts and the cultural-evolution version doesn't.

It also clashes with the rest of my framework. Look at how "resistance" was used in different places:

- In Chapter 7, **high resistance** meant conviction and commitment: deep, stable states, generally good.
- In my language idea, **high resistance** meant anger, conflict, and confusion: generally bad.
- In my violence idea, violence was **low resistance** for certain systems.

The same word meant opposite things in different places, so it wasn't really explaining anything. I'm dropping the physics framing here and keeping the social-science claim.

### Language doesn't encode physics

The language idea doesn't hold up either.

First, it's **circular**. I sorted words into "attractive" and "repulsive," then treated the sorting as evidence for attraction and repulsion. Any set of words can be sorted to fit a theory that way.

Second, psychology already has a well-supported way to organize emotion words. The **circumplex model** (James Russell, 1980) places emotions along two dimensions: **valence**, from pleasant to unpleasant, and **arousal**, from calm to activated. **[E]** "Calm" is pleasant and low-arousal. "Anger" is unpleasant and high-arousal. "Excitement" is pleasant and high-arousal, which my attraction/repulsion scheme has trouble placing.

Third, languages divide emotional experience differently. Some have words for emotions English doesn't name, and the boundaries between categories vary. **[E]** That fits words reflecting culture and psychology, not underlying physics.

---

## What survives

- **Morality as an emergent group-level property is a defensible position**, supported by game theory, cultural evolution, and social psychology. **[E/P]**
- **Moral norms varying across groups and scales** is well documented. **[E]**
- **People behaving differently in groups** is well documented (conformity, deindividuation). **[E]**
- **The collapse-resistance framing is dropped.** It adds no predictions, and "resistance" was used inconsistently.
- **The language-encodes-physics idea is withdrawn.** Emotion words track valence and arousal and vary across cultures.

---

**What would change my mind about this chapter:** a prediction about group moral behavior that a physics-style "resistance" model gets right and game-theoretic or cultural-evolution models get wrong.
