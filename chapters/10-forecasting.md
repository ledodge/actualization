# Chapter 10: Patterns, Forecasting, and Clairvoyance

### Reading trajectories, honestly

---

## The intuition

I've long had a theory about clairvoyance. Some people might be good at picking up the patterns in their own life's curve, or in someone else's, and making highly educated guesses about where it's headed. They might be more sensitive to the forces acting on other people, the way some people seem more sensitive to the "energy" in a room.

That would explain why seers and mediums, Nostradamus being the famous example, are often vague and sometimes wrong. They can see the trajectory, but they can't foresee when a person will interact with someone and change direction. Still, an educated guess will be right more often than an uneducated one.

This chapter keeps the part of that idea that holds up, and it holds up well.

---

## The check

### Forecasting skill is real and measurable

The strongest support for my intuition comes from careful research on human prediction.

From 2011 to 2015, the US intelligence research agency IARPA ran a forecasting tournament on real-world questions. Will this country hold an election by June? Will this commodity's price pass a threshold? Philip Tetlock's **Good Judgment Project** found that a small fraction of ordinary volunteers, whom he called **superforecasters**, were consistently and measurably better than everyone else. By some reports they even beat professional analysts with access to classified information. **[E]**

What made them good wasn't mystical. They:

- broke questions into parts and estimated each one,
- started from base rates (how often this kind of thing usually happens),
- updated their estimates in small steps as new information arrived,
- stayed alert to their own biases.

And their accuracy **fell off the further ahead they forecast**. Beyond about a year, even superforecasters weren't much better than chance on many questions. **[E]**

### The predictability horizon, again

That falloff is exactly what Chapter 4 predicts. Human affairs are chaotic systems, so they have a **predictability horizon**. Within it, someone who reads patterns well can beat chance substantially. Beyond it, nobody can, however skilled.

In Chapter 5's language, forecasting skill is the ability to **extract mutual information** between the past and the future of a trajectory. **[E/F]** Good forecasters get more of it, but there's only so much there to get, and it runs out with distance.

That's the precise form of "an educated guess beats an uneducated one."

### Reading people

The other part of my intuition, sensitivity to the forces acting on others, also has a scientific counterpart. Psychologists study **empathic accuracy**: how well people infer others' thoughts and feelings. Some people are measurably better at it than others, and they rely on subtle cues such as facial expression, tone of voice, posture, and context. **[E]** It's a skill, not a sense for invisible energy, but it's real.

### Nostradamus is a counterexample

Here my original intuition goes wrong. I treated Nostradamus as someone who saw real trajectories but described them unclearly. The evidence points the other way.

His quatrains are vague, symbolic, and undated. That lets almost any later event be fitted to one of them *after it happens*, which is called **retrofitting** or **postdiction**. Nobody has used his writing to predict a specific event *before* it occurred. **[E]**

The same effect shows up in psychology as the **Barnum effect** (also called the Forer effect): people rate vague, general descriptions as uncannily accurate descriptions of themselves. **[E]**

Vagueness isn't a sign of partial vision. It's what makes a prediction impossible to check. Superforecasters do the opposite: they give specific probabilities for specific events, so they can be scored.

### What about genuine precognition?

For completeness: has anyone shown that people can sense the future through some channel beyond ordinary inference?

Researchers have looked for decades. Results that seemed positive, such as some **ganzfeld** telepathy studies and Daryl Bem's 2011 "Feeling the Future" experiments, haven't replicated reliably when tested independently. Bem's work became a major prompt for psychology's broader **replication crisis**. **[E]** The honest summary is that there's currently no reliable evidence for precognition beyond what ordinary information and inference explain.

That doesn't prove it's impossible. But this framework doesn't need it. Everything my clairvoyance intuition got right is explained by pattern-reading within the predictability horizon.

---

## What survives

- **Some people really are better at predicting where things are headed**, and it's been measured. **[E]**
- **Their skill comes from extracting real information from the past**: base rates, careful updating, attention to cues. **[E/F]**
- **Accuracy falls off with distance into the future**, as chaos predicts. **[E]**
- **Reading people is a measurable skill** (empathic accuracy), based on real cues. **[E]**
- **Nostradamus-style vagueness is a warning sign, not partial vision.** **[E]**
- **No reliable evidence supports precognition beyond inference**, and the framework doesn't require it. **[E]**

---

**What would change my mind about this chapter:** a well-controlled, preregistered, independently replicated study showing prediction beyond what available information allows.
