The thought that started it was simple: maybe consciousness isn't a special thing that brains do. Maybe it's an ordinary thing that *everything* does, and brains are just an extreme case of it.

Here's the version I wrote down that night.

## The original intuition

In quantum mechanics, a system can exist in a *superposition*, a combination of several possible states at once. When the system is measured, it shows up in only one of them. Physicists call this *wavefunction collapse*, and what exactly causes it, or whether it really happens, is one of the oldest open problems in physics.

My intuition was that collapse might be the whole story. Every moment, the universe takes a range of possibilities and turns them into one definite fact. A particle settles on a position. A heart beats. A choice gets made. I started calling this process **actualization**, and I wondered whether what we call "consciousness" is simply what actualization feels like from the inside, at very high speed and in a very dense system like a brain.

If that were true, consciousness wouldn't be on or off. It would be a matter of degree. A rock would be "conscious" in the faintest possible sense, actualizing slowly and simply. A human brain would be at the other extreme. Thought wouldn't be the exception in nature. It would be the loudest example.

I liked this idea a lot. That was exactly the reason not to trust it yet.

## Why this book is shaped the way it is

When you have an idea that feels like it explains everything, there are two easy mistakes. One is to keep it to yourself because it might be wrong. The other is to publish it as if it were already right.

This book tries to avoid both. Each chapter follows the same three steps:

1. **The intuition.** What I first thought, in the form I first thought it, including the mistakes.
2. **The check.** What established physics actually says about the topic, and where my intuition disagrees with it.
3. **What survives.** The version of the idea that's still standing afterward, which is sometimes stronger, sometimes smaller, and sometimes nothing.

I'm leaving the mistakes in deliberately. Some of them taught me more than the parts I got right, and seeing *how* an idea fails is more useful than seeing only the polished result. For example, an early version of this framework said a rock should be barely conscious and a brain highly conscious, because brains "collapse more often." When I checked what the closest real physical quantity actually measures, it turned out a large rock collapses at least as often as a brain does. By my own definition, the rock should have been more conscious than me. That didn't sink the whole project, but it forced me to rethink what makes brains different. That's Chapter 9.

## Three kinds of claims

As I worked on this, I kept mixing together three different kinds of statements, and untangling them became one of the most useful habits I developed. You'll see them tagged throughout the book:

- **[E] Empirical** claims can be checked against the world. "Erasing information costs energy" is empirical, and it has been measured.
- **[F] Formalizable** claims could be written as mathematics, but no experiment exists yet that would test them. "The depth of a fact equals the number of independent records of it" is in this category.
- **[P] Philosophical** claims can't be settled by any observation, at least not as stated. "There is something it is like to be a rock" is philosophical. That doesn't make it meaningless, but it does mean evidence can't decide it.

A lot of speculative writing about consciousness and physics goes wrong by sliding between these categories without saying so: an exciting philosophical claim gets presented as if experiments had established it. I'd rather be explicit, even when it makes my ideas look smaller.

## What I'm not claiming

To be clear from the start:

- I'm not claiming to have solved the problem of consciousness.
- I'm not claiming to have overturned quantum mechanics.
- I'm not claiming anything in this book is true just because it fits together nicely.

What I am doing is taking a set of intuitions seriously enough to test them, and reporting what happened in public, where other people can catch what I missed.

## Where we're going

The next few chapters build the tools: what dimensions actually are (and why I had them wrong), how physicists describe a path through time, why some systems are predictable in principle but not in practice, and why *information* turned out to be the most solid foundation for this idea. The middle chapters take on collapse, irreversibility, and time. The later chapters reach the harder questions, consciousness, forecasting, morality, and whether anything might be watching from "above," and I'll be clear about which of those are physics and which are not.

The final chapter is a list of ways to prove me wrong. If you find one I didn't think of, that's the best possible outcome of writing this.
